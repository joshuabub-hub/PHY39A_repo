# A4: Finite-Rod Model and Validity Checks

## Part 1. Apply Fourier's law

For steady one-dimensional conduction through a uniform layer, Fourier's law is

$$
q''=-k\frac{dT}{dx}.
$$

For constant $k$ and a prescribed heat flux, integration across thickness
$\Delta x$ gives the temperature-drop magnitude

$$
\Delta T=\frac{q''\Delta x}{k}.
$$

The supplied Problem 1.3 work uses $q''\Delta x=50\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}}$.
Thus $\Delta T=(50\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}})/k$. The units check is

$$
\frac{\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}}}{\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}}=\mathrm{K}.
$$

Using the conductivities recorded on the supplied calculation sheet:

| Material | $k$ $\left[\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\right]$ | $\Delta T$ [K] |
|---|---:|---:|
| Silver | 427 | 0.117 |
| Aluminum | 237 | 0.211 |
| Steel | 54 | 0.926 |
| Ice | 1.88 | 26.6 |
| Spruce | 0.11 | 455 |
| Insulation | 0.071 | 704 |
| Aerogel | 0.024 | $2.08\times10^3$ |

The very large drops for spruce, insulation, and aerogel are physically
unreasonable as literal predictions: such extremes would invalidate the
constant-property, intact-material assumptions (and could damage the
material). The calculation sheet also notes that ice starting at
$-40^\circ\mathrm C$ would reach only about $-13.4^\circ\mathrm C$, still
below its melting point.

## Part 2. Build the stationary finite-rod model

Let $T_\infty$ be the uniform ambient temperature and define the excess
temperature $\theta(x)=T(x)-T_\infty$. Consider a differential rod segment
of length $dx$, cross-sectional area $A$, and perimeter $P$. At
stationarity, energy conservation requires axial heat entering minus heat
leaving to equal the heat lost from the side:

$$
\dot Q_x-\dot Q_{x+dx}-hP\,dx\,[T(x)-T_\infty]=0.
$$

The side-loss term has units
$(\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}})(\mathrm m)(\mathrm K)=\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}}$;
including $dx$ gives watts. Dividing by $dx$ and taking $dx\to0$,

$$
\frac{d\dot Q}{dx}+hP(T-T_\infty)=0.
$$

Fourier's law for axial conduction is

$$
\dot Q=-kA\frac{dT}{dx},
$$

with units
$(\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}})(\mathrm{m^2})(\mathrm{K\,m^{-1}})
=\mathrm W$. For constant $k$ and $A$, substitution gives

$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{dx^2}-m^2\theta=0,\qquad
m^2=\frac{hP}{kA}.
$$

Here $hP/(kA)$ has units $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$, so $m$ has units
$\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. For a circular rod, $P/A=4/d$, hence

$$
m=\sqrt{\frac{4h}{kd}}.
$$

The differential equation has the general exponential solution
$\theta=C_1e^{mx}+C_2e^{-mx}$. It is convenient to write it relative to the
tip as

$$
\theta(x)=C\cosh[m(L-x)]+D\sinh[m(L-x)].
$$

The two boundary conditions for the finite, insulated-tip model are:

1. **Prescribed base temperature:** $T(0)=T_b$, or
   $\theta(0)=\theta_b=T_b-T_\infty$. The first rod thermistor hole is the
   analysis base; its measured temperature sets the model's base value.
2. **Insulated tip:** $dT/dx|_{x=L}=0$, equivalently
   $\dot Q(L)=-kA\,dT/dx|_L=0$. No heat leaves through the end face.

The tip condition sets $D=0$, and the base condition sets
$C=\theta_b/\cosh(mL)$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\frac{\theta(x)}{\theta_b}
=\frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]}{\cosh(mL)}}.
$$

## Part 3. Make a provisional finite-length prediction

The course rod is 6061-T6 aluminum. I use the ambient-property bracket
$155\le k\le167\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$: evaluating the NIST 6061-T6
conductivity fit over 293–300 K gives approximately 154.4–155.3
$\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, while the Module 7 README uses
$167\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ as its planning value. This bracket is a
prediction input, not a measured conductivity. The NIST fit's data range ends
at 300 K, so the bracket should not be read as a precision characterization
at elevated rod temperatures.

For $h=10\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$, $d=0.011\ \mathrm m$, and
$L=0.283\ \mathrm m$, the circular-rod expression gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= \sqrt{\frac{4h}{kd}}
   = 4.67\text{--}4.84\ \mathrm{m^{-1}},\\
\frac{1}{m} &= 0.206\text{--}0.214\ \mathrm m,\\
mL &= 1.321\text{--}1.371 \quad \text{(dimensionless)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The values $d=0.011\ \mathrm m$, $L=0.283\ \mathrm m$, and the planned
sensor positions $x=(0,\ 0.025,\ 0.050,\ 0.100,\ 0.150)\ \mathrm m$ are
from the Module 7 README; the position uncertainty is approximately
$\pm0.003\ \mathrm m$. The semi-infinite solution follows from the finite
solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{fin}}(x)}{\theta_b}
&=e^{-mx}\,
\frac{1+e^{-2m(L-x)}}{1+e^{-2mL}}
\xrightarrow[mL\to\infty]{x\ \mathrm{fixed}}e^{-mx}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus Problem 4.12's semi-infinite limit is
$\theta_{\infty}(x)=\theta_b e^{-mx}$. To assess its error, define the
relative error in excess temperature against the finite insulated-tip
prediction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\epsilon_T(x) &\equiv
\frac{|\theta_\infty(x)-\theta_{\mathrm{fin}}(x)|}
{\theta_{\mathrm{fin}}(x)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The following values use the two endpoints of the $k$ bracket; entries are
rounded to 0.01 percentage point.

| Planned $x$ [m] | $\epsilon_T$ at $k=155$ [%] | $\epsilon_T$ at $k=167$ [%] |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.000 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| 0.025 | 1.63 | 1.72 |
| 0.050 | 3.64 | 3.81 |
| 0.100 | 9.01 | 9.31 |
| 0.150 | 16.56 | 16.89 |

For a separate check of the tip-boundary approximation in Lienhard Example
4.8 and Problem 4.20, take the tip coefficient to equal the provisional side
coefficient, $h_L=h=10\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$. The exact convecting-tip
solution has

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{conv}}(x)}{\theta_b}
&=\frac{\cosh[m(L-x)]+\beta\sinh[m(L-x)]}
{\cosh(mL)+\beta\sinh(mL)},\qquad
\beta=\frac{h_L}{mk}=0.0128\text{--}0.0133.
\end{aligned}
$$

Comparing this to the insulated-tip solution at the measured positions, the
largest profile difference is only about $0.41\%$ at $x=0.150\ \mathrm m$
(about $0.22\%$ at $x=0.100\ \mathrm m$). The usual corrected-length
approximation gives

$$
L_c=L+\frac{A}{P}=L+\frac d4=0.28575\ \mathrm m;
$$
its profile differs from the exact convecting-tip solution by less than
$0.001\%$ at these sensor positions. The insulated-tip assumption is
therefore a small correction compared with the semi-infinite approximation
error for this rod.

Using a $5\%$ maximum relative error as the adequacy criterion, the
semi-infinite model is adequate at the first three planned positions, but not
over the full measured region: the errors at $0.100\ \mathrm m$ and
$0.150\ \mathrm m$ exceed $5\%$. Even with a looser $10\%$ criterion,
the farthest sensor fails. This prediction depends on the listed positions,
$L=0.283\ \mathrm m$, $d=0.011\ \mathrm m$, $h=10\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$,
the adopted $k$ bracket, and the stated error definition; it is not a
measurement-based validity check.

## Part 4. Make a provisional one-dimensional prediction

The transverse Biot number compares radial conduction resistance with side
convection resistance. For a circular cross section,

$$
\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}=\frac{h(A/P)}{k}=\frac{hd}{4k}.
$$

Its units cancel:
$(\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}})(\mathrm m)/
(\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}})=1$. With the same $h$, $d$, and $k$
bracket,

$$
\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}=1.65\times10^{-4}
\text{--}1.77\times10^{-4}\ll0.1.
$$

Using the usual $\mathrm{Bi}_{\perp}<0.1$ lumped-cross-section criterion,
each cross section is expected to be represented adequately by one
temperature. This radial conclusion is independent of the finite-length
conclusion in Part 3.

**Application — electronic heat sink.** Forced airflow gives the larger
convective coefficient $h$ than still air. Since
$m=\sqrt{hP/(kA)}$, increasing $h$ increases $m$ and decreases the
decay length $1/m=\sqrt{kA/(hP)}$: the temperature excess decays over a
shorter distance along the heat sink.
